The very beginning of Hanoi as a capital city well-known to many nowadays, dates back to the spring of 1010. Emperor Lý Thái Tổ issued the royal edict to relocate the capital to then-called Đại La, which he renamed Thăng Long (lit. ‘Soaring Dragon’). Upon establishing the capital on this prosperous land, the emperor commanded the construction of palaces, citadels and defensive walls, laying the foundation of Hanoi’s early days.
Thăng Long’s urban morphology tied to the rural areas, which was highlighted by strategies of using natural landscapes and spatial features. A primary criteria to assess lands for architectural construction was “nhất cận thị, nhị cận giang” (“First, proximity to markets; second, proximity to rivers”). This placed a huge emphasis on the integration of local rivers into economic activities. The strategy was further evidenced by the “tứ giác nước” (“the Water Quadrangle”), formed by the confluence of three rivers: Hồng(Red River), Kim Ngưu and Tô Lịch. The Tô Lịch river covered two sides of the West and the North, hence the reason why a fourth river is not included in the quadrangle.

With the strategy in mind, the dynasty commenced the construction of Vietnam’s arguably most important architecture in history – the Thăng Long Citadel. The complex was built according to a plan named “tam trùng thành quách” (“the Triple-Walled Citadel”), arranging the planning into three sectors. The outermost sector is the primary defensive fortification of the citadel (called “La Thành”), designed to protect the capital from military threats and prevent flooding. Being made of earth, the outermost ring spanned tens of kilometers in length, ensuring safety for the residence inside. Situated in the middle sector is the Imperial City (called “Hoàng Thành”), where important offices and administrative institutions would be constructed. Between the first two layers is a residential area which comprises agricultural villages, commercial and craft streets, as well as a network of markets and docks. The most invested sector of the whole construction is the innermost sector – the Forbidden City (called “Cấm Thành”) or the Forbidden Purple City (called “Tử Cấm Thành”). This was where the emperor and his family would work and reside for the majority of his terms. To ensure these layers appeared easily accessible for commuters and officials, 21 gates were opened, serving to connect the three sectors into an architectural complex. These gates became legendary, and survived for the majority of Vietnamese history. Some of the notable gate names were Đại Hưng, Đoan Môn, Đông Hoa or Phi Long, all became the pathway of the capital’s key locations.
