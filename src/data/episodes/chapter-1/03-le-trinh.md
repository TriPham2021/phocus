After the expulsion of the Ming Dynasty occupation in 1427, the Later Lê Dynasty oversaw a radical and comprehensive restructuring of the Capital from a Buddhist-patronized, aristocratic lineage-based layout to centralized, administrative, Neo-Confucian bureaucratic urban planning. The truly transformative changes came in the later years of the Dynasty, under the dual governance of the Lê Imperial House and Trịnh Lords,  when Thăng Long remained the political administrative seat of the country while simultaneously expanding into an international commercial hub (known as Kẻ Chợ), deeply embedded in global maritime trade networks.

As mentioned in the previous section, the planning of Thăng Long during the Lý-Trần period (1010-1400) operated in the “Triple-Citadel Structure”, anchored in principles of phong thủy (feng shui) and Buddhist institutional patronage. The urban area was divided into 61 Wards (phường) each merging agricultural communes with specialized craft production. Under the Lê Dynasty, the capital was consolidated into the single Trung Đô Prefecture, renamed Phụng Thiên Prefecture (Phủ Phụng Thiên) in 1469. Under its control were two counties: Quảng Đức and Thọ Xương, which were further divided into Wards. The city was renamed Đông Kinh (lit. Eastern Capital) to contrast with Tây Kinh (the Western Capital, now Lam Sơn Commune, Thanh Hóa Province) which served as a secondary capital and was the ancestral homeland of the Lê Imperial House.

(Fun Fact: The “Hanoi 36 Wards” (Hà Nội 36 Phố phường) expression came into existence in this period, as the city at this point had 36 Wards)

The spatial structure of Lê-era Đông Kinh is thoroughly recorded in the Hồng Đức Map, commissioned by Emperor Lê Thánh Tông in 1467. To validate the Emperor’s Heaven-granted mandate, state ritual structures like the Đàn Nam Giao (Altar of Heaven) were erected, along with the significant expansion of the Quốc Tử Giám (Imperial Academy) and the Temple of Literature. Within the Forbidden City, the Kính Thiên Palace became the centerpiece of the Capital and the symbolic seat of Imperial authority. The grand, 57-meters long audience hall was destroyed by the French in the 19th Century, leaving only the famous Dragon steps visible today.

After the upheaval of the 16th Century, with the Trinh Lords now in power alongside the Le Emperor, Thang Long transitioned into being a premier heart of trade in the region. The expansion of European trade networks positioned Hanoi to become a bustling center of riparian and market commerce. The city became populated with International traders ranging from European to Siamese to Japanese; the period is perhaps the most cosmopolitan in the metropolis’s entire history. Hence, another historical nickname for the city emerged: Kẻ Chợ, aptly meaning “Market Town”.
