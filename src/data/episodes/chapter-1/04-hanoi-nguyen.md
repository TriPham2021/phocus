In 1802, when Emperor Gia Long proclaimed the Nguyễn Dynasty and took the citadel of Thăng Long, the ancient city ceased to be the capital of Vietnam as the Emperor moved his court down south to Huế (then called Phú Xuân). However, Thăng Long-Hà Nội retained its significance throughout the period as it remained the principal administrative and an important military center of northern Vietnam. 

After taking the city, Gia Long spent about three months reorganizing the city’s governing apparatus as it was transformed from the imperial seat into a fortified military-administrative complex. This political and functional change drastically impacted the architecture of the city. The old Thăng Long was built around a sprawling palace, ceremonial and residential complex centered around the Imperial and Forbidden City. The new empire no longer required an enormous imperial complex of this capacity in Hanoi, and it was to be demolished and replaced with a Vauban-styled fortress.

From 1803 to 1805 came Gia Long’s most dramatic intervention, the demolition of the Lê-era citadel. The massive walls of the old fortress were replaced by a compact fort modeled after European Vauban military architecture, featuring angled ramparts and five heavily fortified gates. Large portions of the Lê-era enclosure disappeared, while the remaining administrative and military functions still held by Thăng Long were concentrated within the smaller fortress.

One of the most iconic buildings of Nguyễn-era Hà Nội still standing today was part of the new compact citadel: the Kỳ Đài or Hanoi Flag Tower. It was constructed in 1805 as part of the new series of fortifications, on the foundation of the demolished Tam Môn gate on the south of the former Forbidden City. The 33.4 m high octagonal tower served as an observation point from within the fortress. Unlike the rest of the fortress, the tower survived French-era demolition and went on to become one of the most visible remnants of Nguyễn-era Thăng Long-Hà Nội, an example of its practical defensive structures.

However, not all of Lê-era Thăng Long-Hà Nội was destroyed by the Nguyễn-era reconstructions. Kính Thiên Palace, the heart of the old Forbidden City, actually held Emperor Gia Long’s court in 1802 and was even expanded in the rear in 1804 but eventually rebuilt in 1816 due to deteriorating wood conditions. During the Nguyễn era, the palace held continued primacy as the imperial residence during visits to the North.

Ultimately, the Nguyễn-era reconstructions demonstrate how a change in administrative function fundamentally changed the architecture of Thăng Long-Hà Nội, from the seat of imperial power to a reduced but still significant modernized administrative-military stronghold in Northern Vietnam.
